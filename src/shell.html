How this works

The idea

Real college football sorts teams by money and history. A program can lose for a decade and keep its seat; a program can win for a decade and never get one. This is the same sport sorted by results instead: two tiers, and every second year the floor of the top tier trades places with the ceiling of the one below.

Everything downstream follows from that. If teams are going to move between tiers, the tiers need fixed sizes, the divisions need to be worth winning, and the schedule needs to be fair enough that relegation feels earned rather than drawn.

Two tiers

  • Tier I — the current FBS, 138 teams at the start.
  • Tier II — the current FCS, 128 teams at the start.

Tier I settles at 128 after the first cycle, eight divisions of sixteen. Tier II floats above that, since it absorbs whoever comes down.

Conferences are geography

Four conferences, drawn by clustering every team's campus on an equal-area projection and forcing the four groups to equal size. No history, no brand, no invitations: Northeast, Southeast, Central and Western.

Equal size is the constraint that hurts. The Southeast has about 41 teams in a 35-seat conference, so the overflow pushes west — which is why Iowa and Illinois sit in Central and Minnesota and Nebraska sit in Western. Where geography allowed, rival pairs were kept inside the same conference; 107 of 116 pairs made it.

Tier II uses the same four footprints, so a team that changes tier never changes region.

Divisions are strength

Each conference splits into an A and a B half, and those halves are built for equal strength rather than equal geography. The split matches average rating and also balances how many top-quartile, middle and bottom teams land on each side, so one division cannot be a graveyard while the other is a gauntlet.

At the opening alignment, no conference's halves differ by more than 0.05 rating points. Because the halves drift as teams move between tiers, each reshuffle makes the fewest swaps needed to bring them back within 1.5 points.

Twelve games

  • 7 or 8 division games, alternating across the two seasons of a cycle.
  • 3 crossover games against the other half of the conference, rotating yearly.
  • The rest non-conference, matched on rating and nudged to even out schedule strength.

The division slate is a full round robin split in half by circulant offsets: one season takes the low offsets, the next takes the rest. Together they cover every pairing, so every team plays its entire division once per cycle. Everyone in a division plays the same number of division games.

Rivalries

One rival per team, claimed by the biggest games first — which is why Michigan–Ohio State and Alabama–Auburn survive while Michigan–Michigan State and Alabama–LSU do not. 232 of 266 teams end up with a rival.

A rivalry is played three years in four, with the off year staggered per pairing. If the pair already meet on the division slate, that is the game; if not, it is added on top and spends an outside slot. Rivalry games never displace anyone's division allotment.

The playoff

Twenty-four teams: eight division winners and sixteen at-large.

  • Round one — the four conference championships and eight at-large play-in games.
  • Seeds 1–4 go to conference champions, 5–8 to the teams they beat, 9–16 to the play-in winners.
  • Then a fixed sixteen-team bracket: round of 16, quarterfinals, semifinals, final.

Bowls

The whole real slate is in play, and none of it is fixed to a round. The six majors — Rose, Sugar, Orange, Cotton, Fiesta and Peach — rotate every year: two host semifinals, four host quarterfinals, and which is which shifts annually. Sixteen of the remaining bowls rotate into the play-in and the round of 16.

Whatever is left over is still played. The best teams that missed the field are paired off and sent to the bowls the playoff did not claim, roughly twenty games a year, so a nine-win team that fell short of the twenty-four still has somewhere to go. Those games count for nothing toward promotion.

Conference championships move around the membership, one campus a year. The title game rotates through twenty NFL buildings. Tier II runs a parallel slate under its own names.

Relegation

The worst combined two-year record in each division goes down. The first cycle is relegation only — ten teams drop so Tier I lands on 128 — and after that it is one down and one up per division.

Ties break in this order:

  1. Two-year record
  2. Two-year conference record
  3. Head to head over the two years
  4. Point differential, capped at ±21 a game
  5. Strength of schedule
  6. Playoff ranking
  7. Random draw

The cap on margin is deliberate. Without it, a team fighting the drop could bury weak opponents to buy its way clear; with it, a 45-point win is worth the same as a 21-point one.

Promotion

Going up is not simply the best record. Each Tier II team earns a score across the cycle:

  • 60% two-year record
  • 20% playoff run
  • 10% strength of schedule
  • 10% ranking

The playoff share is measured against a perfect run — reaching the field and winning out — not against whoever went furthest in that division, so a weak field scores low across the board. A team cannot schedule nobody, go unbeaten and let the record alone carry it up.

The simulation

Every team carries a rating roughly reflecting present-day strength. Games are decided by the rating gap plus home field and a healthy dose of noise, so favourites usually win and upsets happen. Ratings drift a little each year and pull gently toward the tier average; you can turn that off, or set any rating by hand, on the Ratings tab.

Ratings are a blunt instrument and the results should be read as a shape, not a forecast.
